03 — About

Self-taught.
Systems-bound.

I started as a self-taught designer: a physics and computer-science graduate who fell into design through the Google UX certificate and never came back out.

04 — How I Work

Understand, then
give it structure.

The loop changes shape for every problem. This is the constant underneath.

Understand

Business goals, users, technical constraints, and the system that already exists behind the screen.

Structure

Information architecture, workflows, states and product logic before any pixels get arranged.

01

The product wasn't one product.

When I joined Intozi in 2023, the company had five or six separate dashboards, one per client, each solving the same category of problem in a slightly different way.

Underneath them all ran the same machinery: AI applications, cameras, locations, events, alerts, servers, users, reports, configuration. The same concepts, rebuilt again and again, drifting further apart each time.

The opportunity was clear: replace them all with one scalable platform that could serve every existing client, and every client after them.

The system underneath
  • AI applications
  • Cameras
  • Locations
  • Events
  • Alerts
  • Servers
  • Users
  • Reports
  • Configuration
The challenge — how do you fold a large amount of technical functionality into one product without making it feel overwhelming?
02

The scale of the thing.

The challenge was never "combining screens." Each part of the platform carried relationships, dependencies and different user needs, and all of it had to work as one system.

5–6→1client dashboards consolidated
0+AI applications supported
30–40clients on the platform
0+live deployments
03

Not too many screens. Too many connected concepts.

An AI application connects to cameras. Cameras belong to locations. Cameras generate events. Events trigger alerts. Servers support deployments. Users touch different parts of the system. Reports pull it back together.

So instead of designing features one screen at a time, I mapped the system itself and let that map drive the information architecture and navigation.

What the map changed
  • Navigation followed ownership, not menus
  • Every object got one canonical home
  • Relationships became links, not duplicate screens
Fig. 06 — Reconstructed system map. Hover or focus any node: every concept connects.
04

Problem 01 — one dashboard, many clients.

Client-specific dashboards made sense when each client was the product. A unified platform needed a different mental model: one experience that could absorb different deployments, locations, cameras, AI applications, roles and configurations without forcing every user through everyone else's complexity.

The answer was a stable hierarchy under a stable global structure: Client → Location → Camera → AI Application → Event. Where you are never changes how you got there; what you see scales underneath.

What I worked on
  • Hierarchy — what owns what
  • Navigation — global vs contextual
  • Context — always knowing where you operate
  • Scalability — 10 cameras or 400
Clienttenant · roles · config
Locationsite · zones
Camerasource · stream
AI Appdetection rules
Eventevidence · alert · report

Fig. 07 — the ownership chain that organised the platform

Design principle — keep the global structure stable, and let content and context scale underneath it.
05

Problem 02 — designing for information overload.

An operations dashboard can't show everything equally, so it stopped trying. I structured the overview around one question: what does this user need to understand the current state of their system?

The hierarchy that answered it
First — system status

Deployment health, connectivity, what needs attention before anything else.

Then — priority alerts

The events that crossed a threshold, ranked, not mixed into the stream.

Then — recent events

A scannable digest of detections, filterable before it's read.

Then — app activity

Which AI applications are running, where, and how hard.

Context — deployment health

Servers and edge devices, without going to a server screen.

Last — quick actions

The three or four things operators actually do from the top.

The goal — turn a dense operational system into a starting point for decisions, not a wall of everything the system knows.
06

Problem 05 — configuration without fear.

Setting up an AI application wasn't a form. It was a workflow with dependencies: application type, camera connections, detection settings, events, alerts, then deployment. The design job was to make a technically complex process legible: progressive disclosure, inline validation, dependencies resolved before they break things, and a review step before anything goes live.

Annotated decisions
  • Progressive disclosure
  • Required vs optional, visible
  • Inline validation
  • Dependencies surfaced early
  • Confirmation · error · success states

Connect camerasone, many, or by location
Define settingsrequired vs optional, validated inline

Deployedge or cloud, with status trail

09

The design system — from screens to a system.

As modules multiplied, inconsistencies crept in: similar interactions behaving differently on different screens. So I audited the product, grouped recurring behaviour, defined foundations, and rebuilt components around them: buttons, inputs, dropdowns, tables, filters, cards, modals, navigation, status indicators, empty states.

The point was never "a component library." It was a shared language between design and engineering: states defined per component, patterns documented, Figma and code using the same names.

How it was built

10

The system gets a redesign too.

v1 — built alongside

The first system grew module by module as the product did. It worked until the product outgrew it.

what broke

Variants drifted. Tokens got bypassed. Patterns that worked once didn't survive contact with hundreds of workflows.

v2 — currently leading

Tokens first. States defined per component. Patterns documented. Figma and code referencing the same names.

11

Design didn't stop at Figma.

I worked with the CTO and a 5–6 person frontend team from product definition to shipped code: technical constraints discussed early, workflows clarified before build, implementation reviewed, edge cases resolved, designs adjusted when feasibility demanded it, and consistency defended across every module.

That closeness was a design tool. It's part of why development-phase errors dropped and delivery sped up: fewer surprises survived to code.

How it showed up
  • Feasibility as a design input
  • Design reviews on built screens
  • Edge cases owned jointly
  • ~20% faster delivery
My role — not to hand off finished screens, but to make sure the product logic and interaction detail survived the trip from Figma to production.

14

What the project taught me.

01

Complexity isn't solved by hiding information.

It's solved by structuring information around user goals and context.

02

Systems matter more as products scale.

A pattern that works once isn't automatically a pattern that works across hundreds of workflows.

03

Enterprise UX means understanding the system behind the screen.

Design decisions depend on backend logic, permissions, data relationships and technical constraints.

04

Small interaction decisions add up.

Filtering, states, hierarchy and feedback make a complex product significantly easier to operate.

Ikshana evolved from a collection of client-specific dashboards into a unified enterprise platform. My role grew with it: workflows, interfaces, design system, engineering, direction.

Documents that feel official.

What being the only designer teaches you.

Own the why

With no one to defer to, every decision needed a reason I could defend: to founders, to developers, to the version of me reviewing it a week later.

Systems are survival

A sole designer can't police consistency by memory. The system is what lets one person design a two-sided product without it fracturing.

Prototype to communicate

A clickable flow ends arguments that a static screen never could. 45+ of them were really 45+ arguments I never had to have.

01

A booking app that was really a trust product.

The client offered at-home physiotherapy across the UAE. The brief said "booking app." The real problem said something else: a stranger enters your home when you're hurting.

Every design decision followed from that: credentials before price, transparent costs before confirmation, respectful time windows, and a calm interface, because in healthcare, calm reads as competence.

What the journey needed
  • Discovery by condition, not catalogue
  • Credentials, visible and verifiable
  • Pricing with no fine print
  • Booking that respects the home
02

The service journey.

STEP 01NeedPain or plan: the entry point is the condition, not a therapist list.
STEP 02DiscoverMatched therapists by speciality, licensing and rating.
STEP 03ChooseProfile, price and availability, comparable at a glance.
STEP 04Book & payTime window, address, payment: three screens, not nine.
STEP 05Follow-upSession notes, plan, rebooking in one tap.
